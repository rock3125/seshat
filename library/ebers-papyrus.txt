The Ebers Papyrus

The Ebers Papyrus is the largest and best preserved of the surviving ancient Egyptian medical texts. It is a scroll of roughly twenty metres in length, written in hieratic script and organised into 110 columns containing 877 numbered paragraphs. Each paragraph is a remedy, a diagnosis, or an incantation, and the numbering is a modern editorial convention rather than an ancient one.

The papyrus is conventionally dated to around 1550 BCE, in the reign of Amenhotep I at the beginning of the Eighteenth Dynasty. That date comes from a calendar note on the reverse of the scroll recording a heliacal rising of Sothis, the star Sirius, in the ninth year of a king's reign. The text itself is considerably older than the copy: several passages are explicitly presented as copies of much earlier documents, and the language of some sections is archaic even by New Kingdom standards. The scroll is therefore best understood as a compilation, assembled from a library of older sources rather than composed at one sitting.

Georg Ebers, a German Egyptologist and novelist, acquired the papyrus in Luxor in 1873, reportedly from Edwin Smith, who had himself obtained it some years earlier. Ebers published a facsimile edition with an introduction in 1875. The scroll has been held at the Leipzig University Library ever since, where it remains one of the institution's best known holdings.

The rubric rule governs the layout of the entire document. The main body of the text is written in carbon black ink, made from lamp soot bound with gum arabic. Red ink, made from red ochre, is used for exactly two purposes: the heading that opens each remedy, and the quantity of each ingredient to be administered. Nothing else in the manuscript takes red. This is not decoration. It is a functional convention that marks what begins a section and what must not be misread, and it is essentially the same information design a modern pharmaceutical label uses for the same reasons.

The contents range across what a modern reader would separate into several disciplines. There are treatments for gastrointestinal complaints, for eye disease, for skin conditions and abscesses, for gynaecological problems, and for dental trouble. There is a chapter on the heart which describes the vessels — the metu — running from it to every part of the body, an observation that is anatomically correct in outline even though the same vessels are described as carrying air, water and waste as readily as blood. There is a section on tumours, and a series of paragraphs on what has been read as depression and dementia.

Roughly a third of the 877 paragraphs involve incantation alongside or instead of a physical treatment. Modern readers have often tried to separate the "rational" medicine from the "magical", but the papyrus itself draws no such line: a spell and a poultice appear in the same paragraph, in the same hand, with the same numbering. The distinction is ours, not the scribe's.

The ingredients named include honey, which is antibacterial and remains in clinical use as a wound dressing; willow, which contains salicin, the precursor of aspirin; and castor oil, garlic, juniper, opium poppy and acacia. They also include a great many substances of no therapeutic value and some of considerable harm, among them lead compounds and various animal excreta.

Its importance is not that it is a good medical text by modern standards. It is that it is a text at all. The Ebers Papyrus survives, and the medicine of the culture around it does not, for the least glamorous reason available: somebody wrote it down, numbered the entries, and kept the scroll.
