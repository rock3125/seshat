# Seshat, Mistress of the House of Books

Seshat is the ancient Egyptian goddess of writing, measurement and the record. Her name derives from the verb meaning "to write" or "to inscribe", and she is attested from the Second Dynasty onward, which makes her one of the oldest continuously worshipped deities in the Egyptian pantheon. She is not a goddess of healing, of the sun, or of the afterlife. Her entire portfolio is the keeping of accurate account.

She is usually depicted as a woman wearing a leopard skin over her dress, holding a palm rib on which she notches the passing years, and crowned with her emblem: a seven-pointed star or rosette beneath a downturned arc. The precise meaning of the emblem is disputed. It has been read as a star, as a stylised flower, and as a pair of inverted cow horns above a seven-rayed sign. No reading has won, and the emblem is old enough that the Egyptians of the New Kingdom may not have agreed on it either.

Her most public role was the ceremony called "the stretching of the cord" — pedj shes. At the founding of a temple, the king and a priestess representing Seshat drove stakes into the ground and stretched a measuring cord between them, aligning the building's axis against the stars. The ceremony is depicted on temple walls from the Old Kingdom through to the Ptolemaic period, a span of roughly two and a half thousand years, with remarkably little change in the iconography. The cord is the instrument of the ceremony and also, in the emblem's lower register, part of the sign itself.

Seshat's titles include "Mistress of the House of Books", which places her in charge of the temple libraries and archives, and "Foremost in the House of Records". In funerary contexts she records the pharaoh's regnal years and the spoils of his campaigns, and in the Book of the Dead she is present at the weighing of the heart, though as a recorder rather than a judge. The distinction matters: Seshat does not decide, she writes down.

Her counterpart and, in later periods, her consort is Thoth, the god of writing and wisdom whose portfolio overlaps hers substantially. The relationship between them shifts over time. In some periods Seshat is Thoth's daughter, in others his wife, and in others his female counterpart in a divine pair. From the New Kingdom onward Thoth increasingly absorbs her functions, and by the Late Period Seshat appears far less often in her own right.

Healing belonged to Sekhmet, the lion-headed goddess who both sent plague and lifted it, and whose priests, the wabau, formed one of the recognised classes of Egyptian medical practitioner. Seshat had no clinical role whatsoever. What she had was the record of what was done, which is a different and in some ways more durable kind of authority.
